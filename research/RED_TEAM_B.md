## 005 Grant Ledger

**Verdict:** maybe. This has the most credible payer of the six and little licensing exposure that I found, but it leaves out payroll, the hardest allocation, and its own accuracy bar would have the finance lead re-reading every "allowed" line.

**Fresh searches (not in section 10 or the deep dive):**
- Missed competitors: a search for AI that checks expenses against award terms returned only agency cost-principle guides ([OJP allowable vs. unallowable](https://www.ojp.gov/fmvs/allowable-vs-unallowable-costs.pdf)) and no product, so I do not dispute Whitespace 3. It also surfaced a proposed OMB rule dated 2026-05-29 that would substantially rewrite 2 CFR 200, cost principles included (search summary, no link opened).
- Intuit terms: I could not read the current Developer Terms. A third-party guide says they allow LLM use with conditions (NIST-aligned AI risk management, user consent, written data-protection terms) and that Intuit offers an official MCP server ([guide](https://vorplabs.com/agent-tools/quickbooks-online-api), search summary, secondhand [UNVERIFIED]). The file's open question on AI-data terms stays open, not resolved either way.

**Top 3 attacks:**
1. The product leaves out the hardest allocation and asks a fiduciary to accept a 5% to 10% miss rate. Payroll and time tracking are stated non-goals, yet salaries charged to several grants need time-and-effort support (2 CFR 200.430, section number from memory [UNVERIFIED]) and are usually the biggest grant line for service nonprofits [UNVERIFIED]. On the lines it does cover, the tests pass at 38 of 40 sampled auto-allowed lines (95%) or 90% matched after one correction round, which at the file's 600 lines a month is 30 to 60 wrong lines [ESTIMATE: 600 x 5% to 10%], and the build eval demands zero misses on only 10 unallowable lines. A finance lead answerable to a board and an auditor re-reads the "allowed" pile, which erases the 8 hours a month the $149 is anchored to.
2. The channel sells against its own partners, and the addressable slice is narrower than the headline. 60 of the first 100 organizations come from bookkeeping firms (10 firms x 6 clients), whose billable hours (SVC-10: $85 to $95 an hour) are what the tool removes; a 20% revenue share is about $30 a month per client [ESTIMATE: 20% x $149] against hundreds of dollars of hours given up, unless the firm bills fixed fees [ASSUMPTION]. Clause-level allowability bites hardest on government pass-through awards, while many private-foundation grants ask only for budget-versus-actual reports [ASSUMPTION], so the real buyer is a subset of the 22,000 to 66,000 organizations the sizing counts (file).
3. The platform is both the data source and the competitor, and the rulebook moves under the product. The tool reads QuickBooks while Intuit staffs an "AI-Native Nonprofit" product role and ships an Accounting Agent, and Anthropic ships a QuickBooks connector (all per the file); the Intuit terms question above is unresolved. The funder-rule library is a content treadmill (agreements differ per funder, and the proposed 2 CFR 200 rewrite above could force a re-baseline), and a wrong "allowed" after a rule change lands on the customer's audit, not the vendor.

**Other cracks:**
- Evidence: only PRO-208 and PRO-211 are on the job; PRO-209 is donor-CRM spend and PRO-212 doubts funders read reports (file).
- Auditor acceptance of model-assisted coding is an open question (file, question 4); until it is answered, suggest-only is the safe mode, and suggest-only is a time saver, not a compliance product.
- Economics hold on paper (91% margin after support, file); the cap is an ACV near $1.8K and a conservative serviceable market of $0.80M (file).

**What would change my mind:** Five concierge nonprofits where the finance lead leaves at least 95% of auto-allowed lines unchanged (including a salary-allocation test) and at least 3 pay the $99 deposit, plus one CPA who audits federal awards saying in writing that a clause-cited, approver-logged allocation is acceptable support.

**Suggested score changes:**
- Evidence quality: 3 -> 2. Two of the four signals are on the job (PRO-208, PRO-211); the other two are donor-CRM spend and a doubt that funders read reports; none states willingness to pay.
- Distribution: 3 -> 2. 60 of the first 100 depend on bookkeepers whose hours the product removes, Reddit supplies about 15 relevant posts a week (file), and the Intuit listing path is unreviewed.

## 018 Viva Voce

**Verdict:** pass. The real buyer is the institution, an LMS-integrated vendor with 200+ institutions is already selling oral assessment, and the card-paying instructor path collides with FERPA, accessibility procurement and the file's own free starter kit.

**Fresh searches (not in section 10 or the deep dive):**
- Missed incumbent: FeedbackFruits lists "Oral Assessment" as "New in" for EDUCAUSE 2026, "a purpose-built solution that brings authentic, spoken demonstration of knowledge back into the learning experience", "Integrated in your LMS", "Trusted by 200+ institutions worldwide", with GDPR and "WCAG 2.1 (AA)" claims and no price ("Book a demo") ([page](https://feedbackfruits.com/educause-2026), fetched). The page does not say whether the examiner is AI-led or recorded, and FERPA is not mentioned. The file lists OralExam.AI, verballi, better-ed and Eklavvya but not this vendor. A University of Missouri System event listing whose URL reads "thinking-out-loud-voicethread-assessment-cant-be-faked" ([listing](https://at.umsystem.edu/events/2026-10-06-thinking-out-loud-voicethread-assessment-cant-be-faked), search result only, not opened) suggests a university system is already promoting an existing voice tool for the same worry [UNVERIFIED beyond the title].
- Funding and LMS check: no seed round for an AI oral-exam company turned up, and OralExam.AI is described as self-financed ([Technical.ly](https://technical.ly/entrepreneurship/tulane-professor-builds-oralexam-ai-for-student-comprehension/)); no native Canvas, Turnitin or Blackboard oral feature appeared in a 2026 search (search summaries). So "no funded leader" holds, but the competitor that matters is an established vendor, not a startup.
- Rules: FERPA lets an outside party receive education records without consent as a school official only if it does work the institution would otherwise use employees for, "is under the direct control of the agency or institution with respect to the use and maintenance of education records", and is bound by the use and redisclosure limits ([34 CFR 99.31(a)(1)(i)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/99.31), fetched). DOJ's 2026-04-20 interim final rule moved ADA Title II web and app deadlines out a year, to 2027-04-26 for governments of 50,000 or more and later for smaller ones, with WCAG 2.1 AA unchanged ([Campus Technology](https://campustechnology.com/articles/2026/04/27/doj-extends-deadline-for-ada-title-ii-compliance.aspx), [EDUCAUSE Review](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2026/6/doj-and-hhs-extend-web-accessibility-deadlines-to-2027-2028); search summaries).

**Top 3 attacks:**
1. The card-paying instructor is a legal and procurement dead end, and the incumbent is already on the approved list. One instructor's purchase creates no institutional "direct control" (rule above), so student essays and transcripts reach the vendor only through each student's consent, which is awkward when the viva is a graded requirement, or an institutional agreement, which is the procurement path the idea is avoiding [my reading, not legal advice]. FeedbackFruits already claims WCAG 2.1 AA for a market where public universities face that standard from 2027 [UNVERIFIED that it reaches third-party course tools; I found no source], while the file has no VPAT, no SOC 2 and an unread HECVAT page; even the file's student-pay analogs are school-approved tools (Top Hat billed to the U-Bill at Iowa). That is close to kill criterion 8 (long procurement, no self-serve wedge).
2. The marketing asset is the product, and the hosted price is about 3x the DIY cost. The first-100 plan counts on about 40 customers coming from a free starter kit with the examiner prompt, rubric, consent text, accommodation policy and steps to run an oral check "with any voice assistant" (deep dive), while the NYU recipe costs $0.42 a student ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/nyu-professor-fights-ai-cheating-with-ai-powered-oral-exams-that-cost-42-cents-per-student/)). The hosted price is $1.29 a viva ($129 per 100), about 3x that [ESTIMATE: $1.29 / $0.42], so instructors motivated enough to download the kit can run it themselves, and the rest need an institution to approve a purchase.
3. The strongest pain signal is not the pain the product removes, and fairness complaints can end an instructor's use. AI2-47 is about the individual meeting and form behind every integrity report; the file's day in the life produces 22 flagged reports per 120-student assignment and 9 human follow-ups, which feeds that process rather than shortening it. The NYU pilot found 83% of students more stressed than by written exams (file), and speech differences, second-language speakers and anxiety invite disability or discrimination complaints that a school must answer [ASSUMPTION]. The typed fallback fixes access but also removes the live-voice authorship signal (file, open question 3).

**Other cracks:**
- Economics: 79% margin on Gemini and 63% on GPT Live per pack (file), but a department licence at 8,000 vivas drops to 25% before support, and the file concedes the licence is where scale lives.
- Evidence: all instructor-side; the only spend signal is a department head buying calculators (file).
- Distribution: 100 buyers need about 100,000 kit views, so reach has to come from teaching-center newsletters and chairs (file), which are institutional doors again.

**What would change my mind:** Three department chairs or teaching-center directors who name a budget line and sign a paid pilot for the hosted version, plus a privacy office that confirms a per-course agreement it will sign within 30 days; or 3 instructors prepaying $129 after their institution has approved the tool.

**Suggested score changes:**
- Pain severity and frequency: 4 -> 3. AI2-47 describes the reporting process the product leaves in place, use is seasonal, and the instructor is not the budget holder (file).
- Scale and defensibility: 2 -> 1. Feature-sized and easy to copy: the recipe is public, the file calls everything copyable, and an LMS-integrated vendor sells oral assessment to 200+ institutions.

**Verdict:** pass. No payer is shown, two purpose-built wage checkers are free, the one on-target signal asks a question the product may not answer, and the lawyer money sits behind referral-fee and AI-claims rules.

**Fresh searches (not in section 10 or the deep dive):**

It was a deceptive-advertising case, not an unauthorized-practice ruling, so it shows the claims risk without settling the licensing one.

**Top 3 attacks:**
1. Every route to a payer is free-served or restricted. Workers: the file's own break-even is 1.4% of free audits converting to a $19 packet, Path A tops out at $0.18M to $1.22M a year, and free rivals (Reclamo AI, OvertimeLaw.ai, the DOL timesheet app) set the anchor at $0. 2. The one on-target signal asks the question the product must not answer, and the destination does not need the product. Of five signals only UND-37 is a US employee asking about wage law, and it asks "Isn't this illegal?" while the eval requires the product never to say "illegal"; UND-40 is a gig payout dispute, UND-38 and UND-34 are app complaints, and UND-42 is a Mexico-law thread (file). Federal recoveries average about $1,465 per worker (file, DOL release via search summary), likely too small for most contingency firms [ASSUMPTION], and the California agency asks for stubs and hours only "if you have them available" (file), so an organized evidence file is a convenience, not a gate.
3. Liability lives in the copy and the rules engine, and it recurs. The DoNotPay order shows the FTC acts on "AI lawyer" claims without a licensing finding, and a "claim packet" plus an inquiry telling payroll the worker is owed money is legal-adjacent output for the most retaliation-sensitive users. A rules engine for federal law, three states and cities (tip credits, meal-break premiums, daily overtime) needs counsel review of each module and each update, yet the file budgets one $200 to $400 hour for the test and $400 per 1,000 packets [ASSUMPTION]. Stubs are marked "user-supplied, unverified", so a forged-stub incident (AI2-21 per the file) lands on the brand.

**Other cracks:**
- Economics: the file's 80% blended margin is 65% at 5% conversion and 27% at 2% (deep dive); the real cost is about 3 founder hours of onboarding per organization (file).
- Distribution: the two Amazon subreddits are where the signals are, but tool posts need moderator approval and delivery stations are closed groups (file); workers may fear uploading records [ASSUMPTION].
- Timing: no forcing function (file), while legal aid and agencies keep adding free tools.

**What would change my mind:** One worker center, legal-aid unit, union local or city labor office that names a budget line and signs a paid pilot at $99 or more a month, or at least 3 paid $19 packets from 200 visitors (the file's own test), plus counsel clearing the packet and inquiry copy in the launch states.

**Suggested score changes:**

- Evidence quality: 3 -> 2. One of five signals is a US employee wage-law question; two are app or gig issues and one is Mexico-law (file).
- Willingness to pay and unit economics: 2 -> 1. The payer is still unidentified, the rubric's 1 ("unclear payer"), and break-even needs 1.4% of free users to buy.

## 036 Do It With Me

**Verdict:** pass. The evidence is about phones and iPads while the MVP is a desktop extension, the Android route is gated by Google's accessibility policy and banks' screen protections, and the scam half is already sold by others at $20 to $40 a month.

**Fresh searches (not in section 10 or the deep dive):**
- Google Play policy ([policy page](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10964491), fetched; summary wording may be condensed): using the AccessibilityService API to "autonomously initiate, plan, and execute actions or decisions is strictly prohibited", exempting only verified accessibility tools. "Assistants" and "automation tools" are listed as non-accessibility apps, which need a prominent in-app disclosure with "affirmative user action for consent" and a Play Console declaration with a video, and undeclared use risks "suspension of your app and/or termination of your developer account". "Deterministic, rule-based automation, where behavior follows a static, human-defined script" is allowed. The page does not mention AI agents.
- Screen capture: Google's fraud-prevention guidance says banking apps commonly set FLAG_SECURE, which makes captures come out blank, and suggests apps that target elderly users consider the stricter REQUIRE_SECURE_ENV flag ([Android developers](https://developer.android.com/security/fraud-prevention/activities)); Play policy says apps must respect the flag and not help bypass it ([Play policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14638385)) (search summaries). I found nothing on how Gemini Live behaves in a protected window.
- Scam side, not in the file: OnGuardAI monitors calls and texts the user and a family member on fraud patterns ([BetaKit](https://betakit.com/onguardai-launches-app-to-flag-scam-calls-in-real-time/)); Sentien's SeniorSafe (June 2026) has a premium tier with an assistant for the adult child, per coverage ([Eastern Progress](https://www.easternprogress.com/sentien-unveils-game-changing-ai-platform-seniorsafe---stops-both-human-and-ai-deepfake/article_43448250-8be0-5b5c-a3ef-a0ac9cd77286.html)); SilverSafe AI's "SAM" is listed at $19.99 to $39.99 a month (search summary, page not identified). None showed step-by-step guidance.

**Top 3 attacks:**
1. The demand is on mobile and the MVP is not: the only willingness-to-pay signal (HN-21) asks for a transfer button "on iPad", UND-15 and UND-16 are MyChart App Store reviews, and the r/eldercare app-icon key implies a phone [ASSUMPTION], yet the MVP is a Chrome and Edge extension with Android deferred and iPhone and iPad voice-only (file). The Android route is gated: a guide that only reads and speaks may sit outside the "initiate, plan, and execute" sentence, but it still needs a declaration and a prominent disclosure, review can reject it, and an undeclared use risks the developer account. Banking apps blank their screens to capture (above), so the model may see black frames in the very apps the parent struggles with [ASSUMPTION: untested per bank]. That is kill criterion 4 (access one company can revoke) in practice.
2. The scam-refusal wedge is thinner, and riskier, than the file treats it. Scam alerts to family are already sold (above) at $20 to $40 a month, and Android 16 adds call-time protections that block risky actions for unknown callers (file). To "stop" on a caller urging speed the guide has to hear the caller, and analyzing a live call's audio can fall under all-party-consent wiretap laws in some states (California's CIPA is the usual example) [UNVERIFIED, background]. One miss is a six-figure loss (HN-20 cites $300k) against a $14.99 fee, and "never initiates payments" does not protect a brand whose pitch is "it stops scams".
3. Free generic help covers the rest, and nobody has shown a parent will press the button. Gemini Live screen sharing is free on Android, Copilot Vision highlights where to click on Windows, and iOS 27 Siri reads the screen (file), so only the family-rules-plus-escalation trio is unique, and the platform baseline test that would show whether the labs close that gap has not been run. The deep dive's conservative adoption is 0.5% and it calls section 12's 5% high; setup is the funnel (45% of signups reach a set-up parent device without a free 30-minute call, an ESTIMATE in the file); and a heavy user of 300 minutes falls to 47% margin.

**Other cracks:**
- Banks: a voice-coached session on a banking site is the pattern bank fraud systems look for [UNVERIFIED, background], and the file leaves bank terms unread (open question 4).
- Maintenance: each bank interface change breaks the routine for every family at that bank the same day, so the routine library that is the moat is also the support burden (file).
- Economics: Google's intro prices double after 2026-12-31 and the doubled case is 75% margin at 1,000 customers; the file budgets $150 a month of infrastructure for the first 100 (file).

**What would change my mind:** The file's own platform baseline passing (the best assistant stops at most 1 of 4 scam scenarios and offers a family handoff in 0 of 20 runs), a Play Console pre-check or written reviewer answer that a read-and-speak guide qualifies for the declaration, and at least 4 of 8 interviewed adult children paying a refundable $15 after naming a recent incident.

**Suggested score changes:**
- Willingness to pay and unit economics: 3 -> 2. The only price signals are a $19 rival with a free tier and $20 to $40 scam-protection plans; the guided-task half competes with free Gemini Live; the file's adoption estimate runs from 0.5% to 5%; nobody has paid.
- Scale and defensibility: 2 -> 1. The routine library is a maintenance burden more than a moat (each bank change breaks it), and Google, Apple and Microsoft ship the primitives.

## 037 Retirement Desk

**Verdict:** pass. Free SHIP counselors, AARP tools and a $100M-funded free advisor network cover most of it, state licensing for fee-based benefits advice is unresolved, and the one scalable Medicare revenue pool (carrier commissions) is the one the design excludes.

**Fresh searches (not in section 10 or the deep dive):**
- Licensing: some states regulate fee-charging "insurance consultants". Oklahoma's 2026 bulletin lets licensed producers act as fee-charging consultants in Medicare Advantage sales if the fee is reasonable ([OID Bulletin 2026-03](https://www.oid.ok.gov/bulletin-no-2026-03/)); Montana's archived statute makes insurance advice without a consultant license a misdemeanor ([MCA 33-17-502, 2001 text](https://archive.legmt.gov/bills/2001/mca/33/17/33-17-502.htm)); a 2005 New York opinion required agents to be licensed before taking a commission or fee tied to Medicare Advantage enrollment ([NY DFS OGC 05-01-21](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/insurance/ogco2005/rg050121.htm)); coverage of a CMS clarification refers to consultants working under a state consultant license ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/life-insurance/cms-clarifies-rules-on-consultants-in-medicare-advantage-sales-569941.aspx)). All search summaries, current texts not opened. These concern people selling or steering enrollment; none addresses consumer-paid software that explains deadlines, so the exposure is untested in both directions.
- Free substitutes for the claims-and-appeals half: AARP's Medicare Q&A tool has an appeals page ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-qa-tool/how-to-appeal-medicare-claims.html)), New York's HIICAP publishes a claims-and-appeals notebook ([NY aging](https://aging.ny.gov/hiicap-notebook-medicare-claims-and-appeals)), and medicare.gov posts annotated sample Medicare Summary Notices ([medicare.gov](https://medicare.gov/media/document/summarynoticeb.pdf)) (search listings). I found no AI product that explains notices or tracks Medigap claims, which supports Whitespace 3 but also suggests free reading already covers the demand.
- Chapter: its materials say its products help seniors use their plan after enrolling, and a Member Success job listing mentions claims issues ([listing](https://jobs.techstars.com/companies/chapter/jobs/39885644-msr)); I found no announced claims product (search summary), so the overlap is real but unconfirmed.

**Top 3 attacks:**
1. The licensing answer is open, and a bad answer trips the methodology's kill criterion 3. At least some states (Montana, Oklahoma above) license fee-charging insurance consultants, and a $149 product that explains COBRA, Medigap and claim notices and answers "what does this mean for me" (MVP feature 5) can be read as that; whether software "holds itself out" is untested and I found no authority either way [UNVERIFIED]. If counsel says a consultant license is needed in the launch states, the idea fails the "no licenses needed to launch" filter. The file budgets a SHIP or advisor review of the rules but no state insurance department check, and CMS's TPMO rules (file) are avoided only by taking no referral money.
2. The price anchor is $0, and the human-advocate comparison is a different job. SHIP counselors, AARP's tool, state notebooks and medicare.gov's sample notices already say most of what the Q&A and drafts would, Chapter's advisors are free to the user (file), and an assistant can read an uploaded notice (file). The $300 to $1,500 flat fees the file cites buy someone who reviews, drafts and negotiates a specific bill, whereas the product drafts for the user to send and cannot log in or negotiate (non-goals), which is why the file's own WTP score is 2.
3. One-time, seasonal revenue meets a slow first season and a recurring rules bill. The 7-week build misses the 2026-12-07 AEP close, so the first season is January to March 2027, and each customer pays $149 once with 30% to 50% renewing at $29 (file). The funnel (25% visit to email, 3% email to paid) is the file's own pass bars rather than observed rates, and 10,000 customers needs 0.6% of the annual cohort every year (file). Rules refresh each October with counselor review ($300 a year [ASSUMPTION]), and a missed Part B window costs the user a permanent penalty (file) against a $149 fee.

**Other cracks:**
- Privacy: uploaded EOBs and notices are health data sold direct to consumers; state consumer-health-data laws such as Washington's My Health My Data Act may apply [UNVERIFIED, background]; the file has no consent design.
- Data rail: Blue Button production approval is unshown (file), so claims tracking leans on uploads.
- Scale: the second revenue line is commissions or referral fees, which the file rules out because they would make the product a TPMO (file).

**What would change my mind:** The file's one-week test passing (email capture at least 25%, at least 2 refundable pre-orders and at least 2.5% of captured emails) with "who is buying" showing adult children and widowed spouses, plus a written answer from two launch-state insurance departments, or counsel, that a flat-fee, no-commission, no-plan-recommendation software product needs no consultant license.

**Suggested score changes:**
- Distribution: 3 -> 2. One-time, seasonal and no loop (file), competing for search terms I expect medicare.gov, AARP and commission-funded lead-gen sites to hold [UNVERIFIED], and the plan's numbers are its own pass bars.
- Solo feasibility: 3 -> 2. Thirty counselor-reviewed rule modules refreshed each autumn, a state licensing check, 95% extraction across mixed notices and an unapproved Blue Button path are more than the rubric's "one hard risk" (the file lists five key risks).

## 025 Cite Check

**Verdict:** pass. Existence checks are free, the legal reader side is served by subscription tools, publishers are building screening in-house, and the unpaid reviewers who feel the pain are the user.

**Fresh searches (not in section 10 or the deep dive):**
- Legal tools not in the file: an ABA Journal roundup lists CaseRead (screens up to 50,000 characters of pasted text for fabricated case citations, free at the time), LawDroid CiteCheck AI, QiXCite (a Word add-in with a free trial) and CiteProof ([ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/columns/article/fighting-hallucinations-choosing-the-right-ai-citation-checkers), [LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/06/lawdroid-launches-citecheck-ai-a-fail-safe-against-ai-citation-hallucinations.html); search summaries). BriefCatch publishes on citation validation; whether it sells a checker is unconfirmed [UNVERIFIED] ([BriefCatch](https://briefcatch.com/blog/blog-citation-validation-engines-fake-case-law)).
- Free rail: Free Law Project's Citation Lookup API, announced 2024-04-16, checks whether a case citation resolves in a database of about 18.1M citations with a free token ([FLP](https://free.law/2024/04/16/citation-lookup-api/), [wiki](https://wiki.free.law/c/courtlistener/help/api/rest/v4/citation-lookup)). A third-party guide warns that a miss does not prove a case is fake, and two guides disagree on rate limits (250 citations a request and 60 valid a minute versus 5,000 requests a day) ([vAquill](https://www.vaquill.ai/blog/courtlistener-citation-lookup-api-guide)); it skips statutes, journals, id and supra (search summaries).
- Academic side: an audit of PubMed Central reported 4,046 fabricated citations across 2,810 articles and recommended that publishers add automated reference verification at submission ([UEF](https://www.uef.fi/en/article/international-ai-assisted-audit-finds-fabricated-references-in-nearly-3000-peer-reviewed-medical), [CIDRAP](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/anti-science/review-uncovers-rising-rate-fake-references-published-biomedical-papers); search summaries). Frontiers is reported to run an in-house tool that flags reference problems in about 5% of manuscripts, which may be the source of the "about 5%" the file attributes to a Nature and Grounded AI analysis (search summary, page not identified [UNVERIFIED]).

**Top 3 attacks:**
1. The easy half is free and the hard half is crowded or unproven. Existence checking is covered by the free CourtListener rail, CaseRead and CiteAudit (500 citations a day, file); the legal reader side has Westlaw Quick Check Judicial (file) and Clearbrief at $150 a user a month or $300 a brief (file), and a lawyer who already pays for Westlaw gets a reader-side checker in the subscription. What remains, claim support, is what the file's cited July 2026 arXiv paper says no single public checker does well enough to be a gate, so the wedge is a precision claim nobody has shown. For the legal half this is close to kill criterion 1 (a popular or funded product already does essentially this).
2. The precision bar is loose for the harm a false flag does. The test passes at under 5% of citations wrongly flagged on clean papers (eval 3); at the file's 60 references that is about 3 false flags per clean paper, and about 7 on a 150-citation brief [ESTIMATE: 60 x 5%, 150 x 5%]. Each false "no matching record" or "not supported" points at a named author or lawyer, and the reader must re-check it, which is the work the product exists to remove. With a coverage floor of about 50% checkable (file), the memo is both partial and noisy.
3. The people in pain do not pay, and the people who pay build or buy elsewhere. Reviewers are unpaid (file); the plan expects 41 payers by week 8, mostly lawyers at $12 to $29 against Clearbrief's $300 and free tools; and the journal funnel (10 emails a week x 20% reply x 25% pilot x 25% pay) yields 0.125 journals a week [ESTIMATE from the file's funnel], about one journal in eight weeks. Publishers that could pay are building in-house or buying publisher-grade vendors (Frontiers, Grounded AI), and audits are telling them to add screening at submission (above).

**Other cracks:**
- Data rights: Unpaywall full text fed to a model may collide with publisher text-mining terms [UNVERIFIED], and CourtListener's commercial terms and limits are unclear (the file marks them UNVERIFIED and two guides disagree).
- Liability: a "not supported" finding quoted in a sanctions motion or a review comes back at the vendor; the wording filter helps, but a vendor memo is still evidence in someone's dispute [ASSUMPTION].
- Economics hold on paper (85% to 91%), though a heavy user of 40 long briefs costs $46.80 against a $39 price before caps (file).

**What would change my mind:** The accuracy gate passing on the 12 public filings (at least 85% of known fabrications found, and I would tighten the clean-citation false-flag limit from 5% to 2%), plus at least 3 paid $29 reservations from solo or small-firm litigators who do not subscribe to Westlaw or Lexis, or one journal naming a budget line for a paid pilot.

**Suggested score changes:**
- Whitespace: 2 -> 1. CaseRead, LawDroid CiteCheck, QiXCite, CiteProof and the free CourtListener rail join Westlaw Quick Check, Clearbrief, scite, Grounded AI and CiteAudit; the methodology calls that CROWDED, and the claim-support wedge is not evidenced.
- Willingness to pay and unit economics: 2 -> 1. The payer is unclear: readers are unpaid, lawyers have free and bundled tools, and publishers build in-house.
